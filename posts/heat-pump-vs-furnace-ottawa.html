By Mike Doucet, service manager at Ashgrove. Mike has been fixing furnaces in Ottawa basements for 19 years.

"Should I get a heat pump or just replace the furnace?"

I get asked this at least three times a week, usually by someone standing beside a 15-year-old furnace, or a few days after their air conditioner gave up in the middle of a July heat wave. Last spring a couple in Barrhaven had two quotes on their kitchen table: one for a new furnace, one for a heat pump that cost almost three times as much. Both salespeople had told them the other option was a mistake.

They weren't wrong to be confused. Half of what you'll read online says heat pumps can't handle a Canadian winter. The other half says you should rip out your gas line tomorrow. For most Ottawa homes, the truth is somewhere in the middle, and it depends on your house more than on anyone's opinion, including mine.

The short answer

If you have natural gas and your furnace still works, the setup that makes sense for most Ottawa homes is a dual-fuel system: a cold-climate heat pump that handles heating and cooling most of the year, with your gas furnace as backup for the coldest stretches of January and February.

If you don't have gas, or you want to stop paying for it, an all-electric cold-climate heat pump is a realistic choice now. Ten years ago I wouldn't have said that.

And sometimes a new high-efficiency furnace is still the right call. I'll get to when.

How a heat pump works (in one paragraph)

A heat pump doesn't burn anything. It moves heat. In summer it works exactly like a central air conditioner, pulling heat out of your house and dumping it outside. In winter it runs in reverse, pulling heat out of the outdoor air and moving it inside. Yes, there's still usable heat in the air at −20°C. It just takes more work to collect. Because the system moves heat rather than making it, it can deliver two to three units of heat for every unit of electricity it uses in mild weather. That ratio drops as the temperature does.

Do heat pumps work in an Ottawa winter?

Yes, if it's the right kind of heat pump.

The heat pumps that gave the technology a bad name around here were built for milder climates, and their output fell off a cliff below about −10°C. I've pulled a few of those out of Kanata basements myself. Today's cold-climate air-source heat pumps are built differently. Many keep producing heat down to −25°C or lower, and some are rated to around −30°C. Even Hydro Ottawa now points out that cold-climate models are designed for exactly the kind of winters we get.

Two things are worth knowing, though:

  1. Output drops as it gets colder. A heat pump sized to heat your whole house at −5°C may only cover part of the load during that one week in February when it sits at −28°C and the wind off the river makes it feel worse. That's why sizing matters, and why most Ottawa installs include backup heat.
  2. Efficiency drops too. On the coldest nights a heat pump is working hard for less return. That's exactly when a dual-fuel system switches over to the furnace.

So the honest answer isn't "heat pumps work" or "heat pumps don't work." It's that a properly sized cold-climate heat pump will carry most of your heating season, and you plan for the rest.

The three setups Ottawa homeowners actually choose

Dual-fuel (heat pump + gas furnace)

The heat pump runs whenever it's the cheaper and more comfortable option, which in Ottawa is most of the fall, the spring and a good part of winter. When the temperature drops past a set point (often somewhere between −10°C and −20°C, depending on the equipment and your energy prices), the system hands off to the gas furnace.

Best for: homes that already have gas and a furnace with some life left, or anyone who sleeps better knowing there's a gas burner ready for a cold snap. It's what that Barrhaven couple went with in the end.

If this sounds like you, our page on heat pump installation in Ottawa walks through what an install involves.

All-electric cold-climate heat pump

A cold-climate heat pump does all the work, with electric resistance backup built into the air handler for the very coldest hours.

Best for: homes without gas service, homes heated by electric baseboards or oil, and owners who want to drop their gas bill and the monthly connection fee entirely. It usually needs a closer look at the electrical panel, and the sizing has to be right.

A new high-efficiency furnace (plus AC)

The traditional route: a 95%+ efficient gas furnace, often paired with a new central air conditioner.

Best for: a tight budget right now, a home where the ductwork or electrical service would make a heat pump expensive, or a furnace that dies in the middle of winter when there's no time to plan.

What each option costs up front

Every house is different, so treat these as rough ranges for a typical detached Ottawa home, before rebates:

Setup Typical installed cost
New high-efficiency gas furnace about $4,500–$7,500
Furnace + new central AC add roughly $4,000–$7,000
Ducted cold-climate heat pump (dual-fuel, keeping your furnace) about $9,000–$16,000
Ductless mini-split (one or two zones) about $4,000–$10,000

What pushes a heat pump quote up: undersized or leaky ductwork, an electrical panel that needs an upgrade, and bigger or draftier homes that need a larger unit. A good contractor flags these before you sign. If the first time you hear about a panel upgrade is on install day, that's a red flag.

One comparison people often miss: if your central air conditioner also needs replacing in the next few years, a heat pump replaces it. You're not paying for a heat pump on top of an AC. You're paying for one machine that does both jobs.

What about running costs?

This is where most online calculators oversimplify, and where I'll be blunt: a heat pump isn't automatically cheaper to run than gas in Ottawa. A CBC report on studies of hybrid systems in Ottawa and Toronto homes found that a dual-fuel setup can actually cost a little more to run than gas alone over a heating season, depending on energy prices, while cutting emissions a lot. Anyone who promises you big savings without looking at your bills is guessing.

Your actual running cost comes down to three things:

  • How efficient the heat pump is at the temperatures you actually get. A model rated for low temperatures matters more than its best-case number on a mild day.
  • What you pay for electricity versus gas, including delivery charges and fixed fees, not just the per-unit price.
  • How well your house holds heat. Insulation and air sealing help every option equally, and they're often the cheapest upgrade of all.

In practice, a heat pump replacing a gas furnace saves the most during the shoulder seasons, from October to December and March to May. In the coldest weeks a dual-fuel system protects you by switching to gas. If you're replacing oil, propane or electric baseboards, the savings are usually much larger, because those fuels cost more for the same amount of heat.

When we do a quote, we ask for a year of your gas and hydro bills and run the numbers on your house. It's the only comparison worth trusting.

Rebates and financing

Ontario's Home Renovation Savings Program offers rebates on qualifying cold-climate heat pumps installed by participating contractors. The amount depends heavily on how your home is heated today. Homes switching from electric heat have been eligible for much more than homes switching from gas. Program details change, so check the official program site (or ask us to) before you count on a number.

For bigger projects, the City of Ottawa's Better Homes Ottawa Loan Program has offered long-term, low-interest loans for energy upgrades, including heat pumps, repaid over many years. Check the city's site for the current terms.

If an all-in quote still feels out of reach, ask for the monthly figure next to the upfront price so you can compare them honestly.

When a furnace is still the better call

I'll tell you to stick with a furnace (or a good furnace repair) when:

  • Your furnace is under about 12 years old and working well. You'll get more value adding a heat pump later as a dual-fuel upgrade than replacing a healthy furnace now.
  • It's January and you have no heat. A heat pump install deserves proper sizing and planning. Get warm first, then plan the upgrade for spring. Every winter someone asks us to install a heat pump during a cold snap, and every winter I talk them out of it.
  • Your ductwork or panel would turn a $12,000 project into a $25,000 one. Sometimes a ductless system for part of the house, or simply waiting, makes more sense.

How to decide: five questions

  1. Do you have natural gas, and how old is your furnace? Gas plus a working furnace points toward dual-fuel.
  2. Does your AC need replacing soon? If yes, the heat pump math gets much better.
  3. How do you heat now? Oil, propane and electric baseboards make heat pumps very attractive.
  4. What do your ducts and electrical panel look like? This is the hidden cost that decides a lot of projects.
  5. How long will you stay in the house? The longer you stay, the more the running costs matter.

The bottom line

For most Ottawa homes the answer isn't heat pump or furnace. It's a heat pump for most of the year, with a furnace or electric backup for the deep cold, sized properly for your house. If your furnace is young and healthy, keep it and plan the heat pump for later. If your AC is dying, or you're heating with something expensive, now is a good time to look seriously.

My own house has a dual-fuel setup, for what it's worth. The furnace kicks on maybe fifteen nights a year.
