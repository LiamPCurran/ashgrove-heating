"Should I get a heat pump or just replace the furnace?" is the question we hear most from Ottawa homeowners, usually from someone whose furnace is 15 years old or whose air conditioner didn't make it through last July.

It's a fair question, and the internet doesn't help much. Half of what you'll read says heat pumps can't handle a Canadian winter. The other half says you should rip out your gas line tomorrow. The truth for most Ottawa homes sits in the middle, and it depends on your house more than on anyone's opinion.

The short answer

If you have natural gas and your furnace still works, the setup that makes sense for most Ottawa homes is a dual-fuel system: a cold-climate heat pump that handles heating and cooling most of the year, with a gas furnace as backup for the coldest stretches of January and February.

If you don't have gas, or you want to stop paying for it, an all-electric cold-climate heat pump is a realistic choice now. It wasn't ten years ago.

And sometimes a new high-efficiency furnace is still the right call. We'll get to when.

How a heat pump works (in one paragraph)

A heat pump doesn't burn anything. It moves heat. In summer it works exactly like a central air conditioner, pulling heat out of your house and dumping it outside. In winter it runs in reverse, pulling heat out of the outdoor air and moving it inside. There's still usable heat in outdoor air at −20°C. It just takes more work to collect. Because the system is moving heat rather than making it, it can deliver two to three units of heat for every unit of electricity it uses in mild weather. That ratio drops as the temperature falls.

Do heat pumps work in an Ottawa winter?

Yes, if it's the right kind of heat pump.

The heat pumps that gave the technology a bad name in Canada were designed for milder climates. Their output fell off a cliff below about −10°C. Today's cold-climate air-source heat pumps are built differently. Many models keep producing heat down to −25°C or lower, and some are rated to around −30°C.

Two things are worth knowing, though:

  1. Output drops as it gets colder. A heat pump sized to heat your whole house at −5°C may only cover part of the load at −28°C. That's why sizing matters and why most Ottawa installs include backup heat.
  2. Efficiency drops too. On the coldest nights a heat pump is working hard for less return. That's exactly when a dual-fuel system switches to the furnace.

So the honest answer isn't "heat pumps work" or "heat pumps don't work." It's that a properly sized cold-climate heat pump will carry most of your heating season, and you plan for the rest.

The three setups Ottawa homeowners actually choose

Dual-fuel (heat pump + gas furnace)

The heat pump runs whenever it's the cheaper and more comfortable option, which in Ottawa is most of the fall, spring and a good part of winter. When the temperature drops past a set point (often somewhere between −10°C and −20°C, depending on the equipment and your energy prices), the system hands off to the gas furnace.

Best for: homes that already have gas and a furnace with some life left in it, or homeowners who want the comfort of gas backup during a cold snap.

If this sounds like you, our page on heat pump installation in Ottawa walks through what an install involves.

All-electric cold-climate heat pump

A cold-climate heat pump does all the work, with electric resistance backup built into the air handler for the very coldest hours.

Best for: homes without gas service, homes heated by electric baseboards or oil, and owners who want to drop their gas bill and connection fee entirely. It often needs a closer look at the electrical panel, and the sizing has to be right.

A new high-efficiency furnace (plus AC)

The traditional route: a 95%+ efficient gas furnace, often paired with a new central air conditioner.

Best for: a limited budget right now, a home where the ductwork or electrical service would make a heat pump expensive, or a furnace that needs replacing in the middle of winter when there's no time to plan.

What each option costs up front

Every house is different, so treat these as rough ranges for a typical detached Ottawa home, before rebates:

Setup Typical installed cost
New high-efficiency gas furnace about $4,500–$7,500
Furnace + new central AC add roughly $4,000–$7,000
Ducted cold-climate heat pump (dual-fuel, keeping your furnace) about $9,000–$16,000
Ductless mini-split (one or two zones) about $4,000–$10,000

What pushes a heat pump quote up: undersized or leaky ductwork, an electrical panel that needs an upgrade, and larger or draftier homes that need a bigger unit. A good contractor will flag these before you sign, not on install day.

A fair comparison point: if your central air conditioner also needs replacing in the next few years, a heat pump replaces it. You're not paying for a heat pump on top of an AC. You're paying for one machine that does both jobs.

What about running costs?

This is where most online calculators oversimplify. Your actual running cost depends on three things:

  • How efficient the heat pump is at the temperatures you actually get. A cold-climate model rated for low temperatures matters more than its best-case number.
  • What you pay for electricity versus gas, including delivery charges and fixed fees, not just the per-unit price.
  • How well your house holds heat. Insulation and air sealing affect every option equally, and they're often the cheapest upgrade of all.

In practice, a heat pump replacing a gas furnace usually saves the most during the shoulder seasons, from October to December and March to May, when it runs at its most efficient. In the coldest weeks a dual-fuel system protects you by switching to gas. If you're replacing oil, propane or electric baseboards, the savings are usually much larger, because those fuels cost more per unit of heat.

During a quote visit we'll run the numbers with your utility bills, not an average. That's the only comparison worth trusting.

Rebates and financing

Ontario's Home Renovation Savings Program offers rebates on qualifying cold-climate heat pumps installed by participating contractors. Amounts depend heavily on how your home is heated today. Homes switching from electric heat have been eligible for considerably more than homes switching from gas, and program details change, so check the program's official site, or ask us to, before you count on a number.

Some utilities and lenders also offer financing on high-efficiency equipment. If an all-in quote feels out of reach, ask for the monthly figure next to the upfront price so you can compare the two honestly.

When a furnace is still the better call

We'd tell you to stick with a furnace (or a good furnace repair) when:

  • Your furnace is under about 12 years old and working well. You'll get more value adding a heat pump later as a dual-fuel upgrade than replacing a healthy furnace now.
  • It's January and you have no heat. A heat pump install deserves proper sizing and planning. Get warm first, then plan the upgrade for spring.
  • Your ductwork or panel would turn a $12,000 project into a $25,000 one. Sometimes a ductless system for part of the house, or simply waiting, makes more sense.

How to decide: five questions

  1. Do you have natural gas, and how old is your furnace? Gas plus a working furnace points toward dual-fuel.
  2. Does your AC need replacing soon? If yes, the heat pump math gets much better.
  3. How do you heat now? Oil, propane and electric baseboards make heat pumps very attractive.
  4. What do your ducts and electrical panel look like? This is the hidden cost that decides many projects.
  5. How long will you stay in the house? The longer you stay, the more running-cost savings matter.

The bottom line

For most Ottawa homes the answer isn't heat pump or furnace. It's a heat pump for most of the year, with a furnace or electric backup for the deep cold, sized properly for your house. If your furnace is young and healthy, keep it and plan the heat pump for later. If your AC is dying or you're heating with something expensive, now is a good time to look seriously.
